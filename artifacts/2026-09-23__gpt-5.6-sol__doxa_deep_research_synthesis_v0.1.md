# Doxa deep-research synthesis v0.1

**Date:** 2026-09-23
**Basis:** user-provided Deep Research report, `Multi-Display Workstations for People With Low Vision`
**Status:** research synthesis; not product requirements

## Core conclusion

The central Doxa hypothesis is credible but not proven.

Strong evidence establishes that screen magnification creates costs when related information cannot remain simultaneously visible: panning, comparison, line/location reacquisition, pointer search, loss of spatial context and repeated movement between work and controls.

What is not established is that adding physical displays is necessarily the best way to remove those costs.

The right Doxa question is:

> Does additional persistent screen space reduce viewport-navigation cost faster than it increases physical visual-navigation cost?

A successful Doxa layout must minimize the **total cost of maintaining context**, including:

- software panning and zoom changes;
- pointer/caret/focus reacquisition;
- working-memory and spatial-orientation burden;
- visual search across displays;
- gaze/head transitions;
- ergonomic load and fatigue.

## Evidence strength

### Established

- Magnification creates meaningful navigation/context costs beyond small-text legibility.
- Reducing those navigation costs can produce large performance improvements.
- Targeted context/navigation assistance can improve performance.
- Added context can simultaneously increase visual load.
- General-population multi-monitor work shows some productivity benefit but also postural costs.
- Low-vision users vary substantially in visual field, acuity, viewing distance and interaction strategy.

### Suggestive

- Magnified Detail + stable Context is worth testing.
- Persistent secondary information may reduce panning/window switching in comparison, reference-based writing, collaborative editing, spreadsheets/forms and communication-plus-work.
- One dynamic surface plus stable secondary surfaces is a strong candidate configuration.
- The laptop may be valuable as a physically closer Detail display for some users.

### Unknown

- Whether two, three or four displays are optimal for low-vision productivity.
- Whether the three upper Doxa panels are sufficient without the laptop.
- Whether the laptop should normally be Detail.
- Whether universal semantic monitor roles are appropriate.
- Ideal Doxa screen angles/distances for low-vision users.
- Display-count dose-response for productivity vs fatigue.
- Whether a persistent physical overview is better than a well-designed compact single-screen interface.

## Important Doxa-specific implication

Three 15.6-inch panels create a very wide visual workspace. At the shorter viewing distances often used by people with low vision, the angular width of the three-panel array becomes especially large.

Therefore Doxa should not default to all displays being visually active merely because they are available.

## Immediate Visual implication

The current two-monitor Visual POC is more than an engineering placeholder: it can become the first experimental platform for the key hypothesis.

The most useful pre-Doxa experiment is a within-user comparison of:

1. one-screen conventional magnification;
2. two-screen magnified Detail + persistent Context;
3. two-screen ordinary extended-desktop use without a dedicated persistent-context behavior.

The third condition matters because it separates the value of **persistent context** from the generic value of having extra desktop area.

Candidate tasks:

- edit/write from a reference document;
- compare web records/products/data;
- spreadsheet entry with distant headers/reference values;
- forms/data transcription;
- document-comment/review work.

Measure:

- task time and errors;
- panning/viewport movement;
- zoom changes;
- pointer/caret loss/reacquisition;
- window/application switches;
- screen transitions and pointer crossings;
- subjective orientation/workload;
- visual/physical comfort.

This would be exploratory N=1 engineering/usability evidence, not generalizable low-vision research.

## Doxa experiment sequence when hardware arrives

1. Context persistence vs conventional magnification.
2. Display-count dose response: 1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 4 active displays.
3. Laptop-close-Detail vs upper-centre Detail vs unrestricted choice.
4. Stable secondary surfaces vs multiple dynamic surfaces.
5. Individual differences recorded rather than averaged away.

Do not prescribe fixed screen roles initially. Let users arrange information, then observe whether patterns recur.

## Product-direction implication

The product proposition should remain a hypothesis:

> Doxa + Visual may improve low-vision productivity by preserving task-relevant information spatially while one view is magnified, reducing the navigation and context-recovery costs of screen magnification without imposing excessive visual or ergonomic load.

The research program should allow that hypothesis to fail. Two screens may prove to be the practical sweet spot for some users/tasks; three may be better for others; four may add no value or become actively detrimental.
